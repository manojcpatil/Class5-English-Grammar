\chapter{Punctuation and Editing: Making Meaning Clear}

\section{Pedagogical Purpose}
Learners have now built vocabulary, sentence, and tense accuracy. Punctuation is the final layer of sentence-level accuracy: it tells the reader how to read a sentence and where meaning begins and ends. This chapter consolidates several punctuation marks met individually in earlier chapters and adds new ones (apostrophe, quotation marks), before learners move on to editing whole paragraphs.

\begin{learningobjectives}
The learner can:
\begin{itemize}
    \item identify missing or incorrect punctuation in a sentence.
    \item explain the purpose of each punctuation mark taught in this chapter.
    \item use commas correctly in lists and after introductory words.
    \item use apostrophes correctly for contractions and possession.
    \item use quotation marks correctly for direct speech.
    \item edit a short paragraph to correct multiple punctuation errors.
\end{itemize}
\end{learningobjectives}

\section{Chapter Opener}
Ms. Sharma has put an unpunctuated note on the board and asked the class to read it aloud exactly as written.

\textbf{The Note:} ``rohans mother said dont forget your lunch box maya asked wheres my pencil box''

\textbf{Rohan:} \textit{(reading confused)} ``It just sounds like one long jumble of words!''

\textbf{Ms. Sharma:} ``Exactly! Without punctuation, even correct words become confusing. Punctuation marks are like traffic signals for readers --- they tell us when to stop, pause, and how to read something.''

\section{Language Experience}
\textbf{A Note Home (Unpunctuated)}

\textit{dear amma i reached school safely today we have a maths test i will come home by four oclock love maya}

\textbf{The Same Note (Punctuated)}

\textit{Dear Amma, I reached school safely today. We have a maths test. I will come home by four o'clock. Love, Maya.}

\section{Discover / Explicit Teaching}
Punctuation marks show the reader where sentences begin and end, how to read them, and what kind of information is inside them. Each mark has its own job:

\begin{table}[h!]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|}
\hline
\textbf{Mark} & \textbf{Job} \\
\hline
Capital letter & Starts a sentence, name, or title \\
Full stop (.) & Ends a statement \\
Question mark (?) & Ends a question \\
Exclamation mark (!) & Ends a sentence showing strong feeling \\
Comma (,) & Separates list items or a pause after an intro word \\
Apostrophe (') & Shows a missing letter or possession \\
Quotation marks (" ") & Shows the exact words someone said \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\section{Grammar Word}
\begin{grammarword}{PUNCTUATION}
\textbf{Punctuation} is the set of marks used in writing to separate sentences and parts of sentences, and to make meaning clear.

\textit{Examples:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item ``Let's eat, Grandma!'' (the comma saves Grandma from being eaten!)
    \item ``Let's eat Grandma!'' (without the comma, it sounds like something very different)
\end{itemize}

\textit{Non-example:} A sentence with no end punctuation at all: ``My favourite colour is blue'' (missing full stop --- feels incomplete).

\textit{Quick Check:} Why does the comma matter so much in ``Let's eat, Grandma!''? (\textit{It shows we are speaking TO Grandma, not suggesting we eat her.})
\end{grammarword}

\section{Core Explanation}

\subsection*{Capital Letters}
\textbf{M}aya lives in \textbf{D}elhi. (sentence start and proper noun) --- contrast with ``maya lives in delhi.'' (incorrect, no capitals). \textit{Check:} Does every sentence start with a capital letter? Are all proper nouns capitalised?

\subsection*{Commas in Lists}
``I bought apples, bananas, and mangoes.'' --- contrast with ``I bought apples bananas and mangoes.'' (unclear where one item ends and the next begins).

\subsection*{Commas After Introductory Words}
``\textbf{However,} Rohan finished his homework late.'' --- contrast with ``However Rohan finished his homework late.'' (missing pause, harder to read).

\subsection*{Apostrophes for Contractions}
``do not'' $\rightarrow$ ``\textbf{don't}''; ``I am'' $\rightarrow$ ``\textbf{I'm}''. \textit{Check:} Does the apostrophe replace the missing letter(s) exactly?

\subsection*{Apostrophes for Possession}
``Maya's book'' (the book belongs to Maya). Add \textbf{'s} for singular owners (the girl's bag), and just an apostrophe after \textbf{-s} for regular plural owners (the girls' bags).

\subsection*{Quotation Marks}
Rohan said, ``\textbf{I am ready.}'' --- contrast with ``Rohan said I am ready.'' (unclear whether these are his exact words).

\section{Types / Classifications}

\subsection*{End Punctuation}
Full stop, question mark, exclamation mark --- marks that close a sentence and show its type. Using the wrong end mark changes the sentence's tone or type entirely.

\subsection*{Comma Uses}
List comma and introductory comma --- commas separate items or set off an introductory word/phrase. A missing comma in a list can merge two items into one confusing phrase.

\subsection*{Apostrophe Uses}
Contraction apostrophe vs. possessive apostrophe. \textbf{its} (belonging to it) vs. \textbf{it's} (it is) --- a very common confusion.

\section{Worked Example}
\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Sentence to punctuate:} ``maya said i cant find my books have you seen them rohan asked''

\textit{Step 1:} Find where sentences begin --- capitalise the first word and any names.

\textit{Step 2:} Identify direct speech --- Maya's and Rohan's exact words need quotation marks.

\textit{Step 3:} Add the missing apostrophe in ``cant'' $\rightarrow$ ``can't.''

\textit{Step 4:} Add end punctuation to each sentence, including a question mark for the question.

\textbf{Answer:} Maya said, ``I can't find my books. Have you seen them?'' Rohan asked.
\end{examplebox}

\section{Compare and Notice}
\textbf{Incorrect:} ``the teachers bag was on the table''

\textbf{Correct:} ``\textbf{T}he \textbf{teacher's} bag was on the table\textbf{.}''

What changed? A capital letter was added at the start, an apostrophe was added to show possession, and a full stop was added at the end.

\section{Independent Practice}
\begin{activitybox}{Independent Practice}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Rewrite this unpunctuated note correctly: ``dear teacher i was absent yesterday because i was unwell i will complete my homework today''
    \item Write two sentences using contractions correctly (e.g., don't, isn't).
    \item Write one sentence with a possessive apostrophe.
    \item \textit{Reasoning:} Why do you think ``its'' (belonging to it) has no apostrophe, while ``it's'' (it is) does?
\end{enumerate}
\end{activitybox}

\section{Grammar Detective}
\begin{detectivebox}
\textbf{Student sentence:}

``my sister ask can i borrow your book maya said yes you can''

\textit{Questions:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item What is wrong?
    \item What should it be?
    \item Why?
\end{enumerate}

\textit{Guidance:} Needs capital letters at sentence starts, quotation marks around the spoken words, and correct end punctuation: `My sister asked, ``Can I borrow your book?'' Maya said, ``Yes, you can.'''
\end{detectivebox}

\section{Common Confusion}
\begin{warningbox}
Mixing up \textbf{its} (possessive --- belonging to it) and \textbf{it's} (contraction --- it is), and forgetting quotation marks around a speaker's exact words.

\textit{How to check:} Try replacing ``it's'' with ``it is'' in the sentence. If it still makes sense, use ``it's.'' If not, use ``its.''

\textit{Correct examples:} ``The bird flapped \textbf{its} wings.'' / ``\textbf{It's} raining today.''
\end{warningbox}

\section{Language in Real Life}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Shopping list:} ``Milk, bread, eggs, and butter.'' (list commas)
    \item \textbf{Text message:} ``I'm on my way! Don't wait for me.'' (contractions, exclamation)
    \item \textbf{Storybook dialogue:} Ms. Sharma said, ``Open your books to page ten.'' (quotation marks)
    \item \textbf{Notice board:} ``Whose water bottle is this?'' (question mark, possessive pronoun)
\end{itemize}

\section{Writing Connection}
Write a short conversation (4--6 lines) between two classmates, using at least one comma in a list, one apostrophe (contraction or possession), and one set of quotation marks for direct speech.

\section{Summary}
\begin{summarybox}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Capital letters start sentences, names, and titles.
    \item Full stops, question marks, and exclamation marks end different kinds of sentences.
    \item Commas separate list items and follow introductory words.
    \item Apostrophes show missing letters (contractions) or ownership (possession).
    \item Quotation marks enclose a speaker's exact words.
    \item Editing means checking a whole passage carefully for these marks.
\end{itemize}
\end{summarybox}
