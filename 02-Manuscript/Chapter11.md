## 1. PEDAGOGICAL PURPOSE

**Why is this concept being taught here?**
After learning about individual parts of speech, students are ready to understand how words and ideas are connected. Conjunctions are essential for building more complex sentences, improving sentence variety, and making writing and speaking more coherent and less choppy.

**What should the learner be able to do with it?**
The learner can:
- Define a conjunction and explain its purpose.
- Identify conjunctions in sentences.
- Use coordinating conjunctions to join words, phrases, and simple sentences.
- Combine simple sentences into compound sentences using conjunctions.

## 4. LANGUAGE EXPERIENCE (LET'S BEGIN)

Ms. Sharma is talking to Maya and Rohan about their plans for the weekend.

**Ms. Sharma:** "What are your plans for Saturday?"

**Rohan:** "I will play football. I will watch a movie."

**Maya:** "I will read a book. I will draw a picture."

**Ms. Sharma:** "Those are good plans! Rohan, can you tell me your plans in one sentence?"

**Rohan:** "I will play football **and** I will watch a movie."

**Ms. Sharma:** "Excellent! And Maya, what about you?"

**Maya:** "I will read a book **or** I will draw a picture."

**Ms. Sharma:** "Perfect! The words 'and' and 'or' are very useful. They help us join words or sentences together. These joining words are called **Conjunctions**."

---

## 5. CONCEPT DISCOVERY (WHAT IS A CONJUNCTION?)

A **conjunction** is a word that joins words, phrases, or sentences together.

Conjunctions act like bridges, connecting different parts of a sentence.

*Example:* Rohan **and** Maya are friends.
-   `and` joins the words `Rohan` and `Maya`.

*Example:* I like to read **but** I don't like to write.
-   `but` joins two sentences.

---

## 6. CONCEPT EXPLANATION (COORDINATING CONJUNCTIONS)

**Coordinating conjunctions** join words, phrases, or sentences that are of equal importance. They connect elements that are grammatically similar.

There are seven coordinating conjunctions, and you can remember them with the acronym **FANBOYS**:

-   **F**or (shows reason)
-   **A**nd (adds information)
-   **N**or (shows negative choice)
-   **B**ut (shows contrast)
-   **O**r (shows choice)
-   **Y**et (shows contrast, similar to 'but')
-   **S**o (shows result)

---

## 7. WORKED EXAMPLE

**Ms. Sharma:** "Let's see how we can use these conjunctions to join two simple sentences into one longer, smoother sentence."

**Sentences:** `Rohan wants to play. It is raining outside.`

**Let's Think Step-by-Step:**

1.  **Read the two ideas:** The first idea is wanting to play. The second idea is that it's raining.
2.  **Find the relationship:** These two ideas are in contrast. Rohan wants to play, but the rain is a problem.
3.  **Choose the right conjunction:** The best conjunction to show contrast is **but**.
4.  **Combine the sentences:** Place a comma and the conjunction between the two ideas.

**Combined Sentence:** `Rohan wants to play, but it is raining outside.`

## 10. GRAMMAR DETECTIVE (FIND THE MISTAKES!)

**Leo:** (Squawks) "My sentences are the best! I used joining words!"

1.  I wanted to play outside, **so** it was raining.
2.  Maya is tall, **or** Rohan is short.

**Ms. Sharma:** "Good try, Leo! You used conjunctions, but you picked the wrong ones. The conjunction must show the correct relationship between the ideas."

**Let's investigate:**

-   In sentence 1, "wanting to play" and "it was raining" are contrasting ideas. The word **so** shows a result. The correct word to show contrast is **but**.
    -   *Correct:* I wanted to play outside, **but** it was raining.

-   In sentence 2, "Maya is tall" and "Rohan is short" are two separate, contrasting facts. The word **or** shows a choice. The correct word to add information or show contrast here is **and** or **but**.
    -   *Correct:* Maya is tall, **and** Rohan is short. (Adds two facts)
    -   *Also Correct:* Maya is tall, **but** Rohan is short. (Highlights the contrast)

**Your Turn, Detective!**

Correct the conjunctions in these sentences.

1.  He did not study, but he failed the exam.
2.  I will have a sandwich, and I will have a salad. I can only choose one.

## 11. WRITING & SPEAKING CONNECTION

**Activity D: Sentence Chain**

Work with a partner.

1.  **Student A:** Start a simple sentence. (e.g., "I went to the market...")
2.  **Student B:** Add a conjunction and a new idea. (e.g., "...and I bought some apples.")
3.  **Student A:** Add another conjunction and a new idea. (e.g., "...but I forgot to buy bananas.")
4.  Keep the chain going for as long as you can!

**Activity E: My Weekend**

Write a short paragraph (3-5 sentences) about what you did last weekend. Use at least three different conjunctions (like `and`, `but`, `or`, `so`). Underline the conjunctions you use.

*Example:*
Last weekend, I wanted to go to the park, **but** it was too hot. **So**, I stayed home **and** read a book. Later, I could watch a movie **or** play a game with my sister.

## 13. LET'S SUMMARISE

In this chapter, we learned that **conjunctions** are joining words.

-   They connect words, phrases, and sentences.
-   **Coordinating Conjunctions** join equal parts. You can remember them with **FANBOYS**.

| Conjunction | Job | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **F**or | Shows a reason | I was tired, for I had walked a long way. |
| **A**nd | Adds information | I like apples and bananas. |
| **N**or | Shows a negative choice | He does not play, nor does he study. |
| **B**ut | Shows contrast | It is sunny, but it is cold. |
| **O**r | Shows a choice | Do you want tea or coffee? |
| **Y**et | Shows contrast | He is rich, yet he is unhappy. |
| **S**o | Shows a result | It was raining, so we stayed inside. |

> **Key Idea:** Using conjunctions makes your writing flow better and helps you express more complex ideas in a single sentence.

## 22. TEACHER SUPPORT NOTE

Likely misconception: Students often default to "and" for every combination, even when "but," "so," or "because" would show the relationship between ideas more accurately.

Support strategy: Use the FANBOYS relationship chart from Section 13 as a quick-reference wall chart, and ask "what relationship am I really showing?" before choosing a conjunction.

Extension: Briefly preview subordinating conjunctions (because, when, although) as a bridge to more complex sentence-joining in later chapters.

Challenge: Ask advanced learners to combine three simple sentences into one, using two different conjunctions correctly.
