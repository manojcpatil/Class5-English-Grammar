## 1. PEDAGOGICAL PURPOSE

**Why is this concept being taught here?**
Learners have already built a single well-organised paragraph around one main idea (Chapter 24) and learned to speak and write with an audience in mind (Chapter 23). This chapter takes both skills and points them at real, functional writing: the kind of letters, messages, and notices people actually write and read every day. Unlike descriptive writing, functional writing is judged mainly by whether it does its job — informs, requests, or announces clearly, in the right format, for the right reader.

**What should the learner be able to do with it?**
The learner can:
- Explain the purpose and typical reader of a letter, a message, and a notice.
- Write an informal letter using the correct parts (date, greeting, body, closing, signature).
- Write a short, complete message containing only the essential information.
- Write a school notice using a heading, clear body, and issuing authority.
- Choose the most suitable format (letter, message, or notice) for a given situation.

## 7. DISCOVER / EXPLICIT TEACHING

### Explicit explanation

Different situations call for different kinds of writing. Each format below has its own job and its own shape:

| Format | Purpose | Typical Reader | Typical Length |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Letter** | To share news, feelings, or a request personally | One person you know (often family or a friend) | Several sentences to a few short paragraphs |
| **Message** | To pass on essential information quickly | Someone who needs to know something *right now* | One to three sentences |
| **Notice** | To inform a whole group of people at once | A class, a school, or the public | A few clear lines, often with a heading |

---

## 8. GRAMMAR WORD

## Grammar Word: INFORMAL LETTER

An **informal letter** is a personal letter written to someone you know well, such as a family member or friend, using a friendly, warm tone and a fixed set of parts: the sender's address and date, a greeting, a body, a closing phrase, and a signature.

### Examples

- Greeting: "Dear Nani,"
- Closing: "With love, Maya" or "Your loving grandson, Rohan"

### Non-examples / Contrast

- A letter to the Principal requesting leave would use a more formal greeting ("Respected Sir/Madam") and closing ("Yours sincerely") — this chapter focuses only on the **informal** letter, written to someone you know personally.

### Quick Check

Which closing suits an informal letter to a cousin: "Yours sincerely" or "With love"? *("With love" — it matches the warm, personal tone of an informal letter.)*

## 9. CORE EXPLANATION

**The parts of an informal letter**
→ Example: address and date (top right), greeting ("Dear ___,"), body (the message itself), closing phrase ("With love," / "Your friend,"), signature (first name only).
→ Contrast: A notice has no personal greeting or closing at all — it simply states the information and names the issuing authority.
→ Check: Does the letter include all five parts, in the right order, and does the greeting match the closing in warmth?

**Writing a complete message**
→ Example: "Mum — gone to Rohan's house to finish our project. Back by 6 pm. — Maya" answers *where, why,* and *when.*
→ Contrast: "Mum — gone out. — Maya" is too short — it leaves the reader worried, because it does not say where, why, or when she will be back.
→ Check: If I imagine the reader finding only this note, do they know everything they need to know?

**Writing a clear notice**
→ Example: a heading (often the word "NOTICE"), the date, a short body stating exactly what, when, and where, and the issuing authority at the end (e.g., "— Principal, Greenwood School").
→ Contrast: A notice that buries the key detail (the date of an event) in the middle of a long sentence makes readers search for information that should be easy to find.
→ Check: Could someone glance at this notice for five seconds and still know the most important fact?

**Choosing the right format**
→ Example: Telling your whole class that the library is closed tomorrow → a **notice** (many readers, one shared fact).
→ Contrast: Telling your best friend you are sorry you missed their birthday → a **letter** (one reader, personal feeling).
→ Check: Am I writing to one person I know personally (letter), to whoever finds this note (message), or to a whole group at once (notice)?

## 10. TYPES / CLASSIFICATIONS

### Parts of an Informal Letter

Name: The five parts
Meaning: Address and date, greeting, body, closing phrase, signature — the fixed structure of an informal letter.
Example: "12 Lake Road, Chennai. / 14 August 2026" → "Dear Nani," → (the news) → "With love," → "Maya"
Contrast: Leaving out the date is a common shortcut, but it means the reader has no record of when the letter was written — useful information, especially for letters kept as keepsakes.
Quick Check: Which part of the letter usually appears first, top right? *(the sender's address and the date)*

### Essential Information in a Message

Name: The message checklist
Meaning: A short message should usually answer *who it is for, what is happening, where, and when* — only the details the reader truly needs.
Example: "Dad — the plumber is coming at 4 pm to fix the tap. Please let him in if I'm not back. — Rohan"
Contrast: Adding unnecessary detail ("...the tap has been dripping since Tuesday and it's really annoying...") makes a message slower to read without adding anything the reader needs to act on.
Quick Check: What four questions should a good message usually answer? *(who it's for, what, where, when)*

### Parts of a School Notice

Name: Heading, body, and issuing authority
Meaning: A notice usually opens with a heading (often "NOTICE") and a date, states the key information clearly in the body, and closes by naming who issued it.
Example: "NOTICE / Date: 14 August 2026 / [clear body] / — Principal, Greenwood School"
Contrast: A notice without an issuing authority leaves readers unsure whether the information is official or just a rumour.
Quick Check: Why does a notice usually end with a name or title rather than a friendly closing like "With love"? *(It is meant for a whole group and needs to show who is responsible for the information, not express personal feeling.)*

## 11. WORKED EXAMPLE

**Task:** Write a short informal letter from Rohan to his friend Anya, who has moved to a new city, telling her about a class trip.

Step 1: Choose the correct format. Rohan is writing to one person he knows personally, so this should be an **informal letter**, not a message or notice.

Step 2: Add the address and date at the top right.

Step 3: Choose a warm, personal greeting: "Dear Anya,"

Step 4: Write the body — share the news clearly, in a friendly tone.

Step 5: Choose a closing phrase that matches the friendly tone, then sign with a first name only.

Answer:

> 45 Hill View,
> Bengaluru.
> 14 August 2026
>
> Dear Anya,
>
> I hope you are settling in well at your new school! I wanted to tell you about our class trip to the science museum last week. We saw a real rocket engine, and Ms. Sharma let us try a hands-on experiment about magnets. I wished you were there with us.
>
> Write back soon and tell me about your new school!
>
> Your friend,
> Rohan

Why: The letter includes all five parts in the correct order, uses a warm and personal tone throughout, and shares one clear piece of news rather than a long list of unrelated facts.

## 12. COMPARE AND NOTICE

**Incomplete message:** "Went to the library. Back later. — Maya"
**Complete message:** "Mum — went to the school library to return a book. Back by 5 pm. — Maya"

What changed? The complete version names who the message is for, says exactly *which* library and *why*, and gives a specific time instead of the vague "later."
Why? A message should let the reader know everything they need to know without having to guess or worry.

## 16. COMMON CONFUSION

### WATCH OUT!

Common confusion: Learners often write a notice in the same warm, personal tone as a letter (for example, starting a school notice with "Hi everyone!" and ending it with "Lots of love"), or write a message so briefly that it leaves out essential details like *where* or *when*.

How to check: For a notice, ask, "Is this addressed to a group, stated clearly, and signed with a title or role rather than a personal closing?" For a message, ask, "Have I answered who, what, where, and when?"

Correct examples: A notice ends with "— Principal, Greenwood School," not "Lots of love." A message says "Back by 6 pm," not just "Back later."

## 25. SUMMARY

- A **letter** shares personal news or feelings with one known reader, using address and date, greeting, body, closing, and signature.
- A **message** passes on essential information quickly — it should answer who, what, where, and when.
- A **notice** informs a whole group at once, using a heading, a clear body, and an issuing authority.
- Choosing the right format depends on your **purpose** and your **audience**.

## 30. TEACHER SUPPORT NOTE

Likely misconception: Students often either write letters that are too brief to feel personal, or write notices/messages that borrow the warm, personal tone of a letter (starting a notice with "Hi everyone!" or ending a message with a full "With love" closing), because letters are usually the most familiar format from earlier schooling.

Support strategy: Use a simple sorting warm-up before writing — show three or four real-life scenarios and have students call out "letter," "message," or "notice" before any writing begins, to firm up the purpose/audience distinction first.

Extension: Introduce the basic idea of a formal letter (e.g., a leave application to the Principal) as a brief preview, without teaching its full format at this level — that is reserved for a later grade.

Challenge: Ask advanced learners to take one real event (such as a class trip) and write it up in all three formats — letter, message, and notice — then discuss in pairs what changed in each version and why.
