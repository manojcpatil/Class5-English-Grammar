## 1. PEDAGOGICAL PURPOSE

**Why is this concept being taught here?**
Learners can now build a single well-organised, vividly described paragraph (Chapter 24) and write for a real audience in the correct functional format (Chapter 25). This chapter turns those same skills toward **creative writing** — inventing and telling a story of their own. It draws together descriptive detail (Chapter 24), tense consistency in narrative (Chapter 15), and dialogue punctuation (Chapters 16 and 22), giving learners a structured way to plan a story before they write it, rather than starting with a blank page.

**What should the learner be able to do with it?**
The learner can:
- Identify the basic elements of a story: character, setting, problem or event, and ending.
- Plan a short story using a simple beginning-middle-end structure.
- Write a story from a set of pictures or a given opening line.
- Use sequencing words to show the order of events clearly.
- Write natural-sounding dialogue within a story, punctuated correctly.
- Write a short diary entry in a personal voice, using consistent past tense.

## 5. LANGUAGE EXPERIENCE

**Two Versions of the Same Story Idea**

> **Version A (list-like):** A boy found a key. He saw a gate. He opened it. He went in. The end.

> **Version B (planned and told):** One rainy afternoon, Arjun found a small brass key half-buried in the garden soil. Curious, he followed the old stone wall until he reached a gate he had never noticed before. His hands trembled slightly as the key turned with a soft click. Slowly, he pushed the gate open — and gasped. Inside was a garden of flowers he had never seen before, glowing faintly in the grey afternoon light.

## 7. DISCOVER / EXPLICIT TEACHING

### Explicit explanation

Every story, however short, is usually built from four basic elements:

| Element | Question It Answers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Character | Who is the story about? | Arjun, a curious boy |
| Setting | Where and when does it happen? | An old garden, one rainy afternoon |
| Problem / Event | What happens that the story is really about? | Finding a mysterious locked gate |
| Ending | How is the problem resolved, or how does the story finish? | The gate opens onto a strange, glowing garden |

Planning these four elements **before** writing helps a story feel complete and connected, rather than like a list of separate actions.

## 8. GRAMMAR WORD

## Grammar Word: NARRATIVE

A **narrative** is a piece of writing that tells a story — it has characters, a setting, and a sequence of events that are connected to one another, usually leading to some kind of ending or resolution.

### Examples

- "One stormy night, a fisherman named Kabir spotted a strange light far out at sea..."
- A diary entry describing what happened to you yesterday is also a narrative, told about real events instead of invented ones.

### Non-examples / Contrast

- A shopping list or a school notice is not a narrative — it has no characters or connected sequence of events, only a list of separate facts.

### Quick Check

Which of these is a narrative: a school notice about a holiday, or a diary entry about your weekend? *(the diary entry — it tells a sequence of events)*

## 9. CORE EXPLANATION

**Planning before writing**
→ Example: Before writing, jot down one line each for character, setting, problem/event, and ending.
→ Contrast: Writing without a plan often produces a list of actions ("He did this. Then he did that.") rather than a connected story.
→ Check: Could I explain my story's character, setting, problem, and ending in four short sentences before I start writing it in full?

**Sequencing a story clearly**
→ Example: "**First**, dark clouds gathered. **Then**, the wind grew stronger. **Finally**, the rain began to fall."
→ Contrast: A story with no sequencing words at all can feel like a jumble of events with no clear order, even if each sentence is correct.
→ Check: Does the reader always know what happened first, next, and last?

**Writing dialogue within a story**
→ Example: "I'm scared," whispered Maya, gripping her brother's hand.
→ Contrast: Writing dialogue without quotation marks ("I'm scared, whispered Maya") makes it unclear that these are the character's exact spoken words.
→ Check: Have I used quotation marks around the exact words spoken, as you learned in Chapter 16?

**Writing a diary entry**
→ Example: "Today was the most exciting day of my summer holidays. I woke up early because..." (personal voice, first person, usually past tense for what already happened)
→ Contrast: A diary entry that suddenly shifts into present or future tense without reason breaks the tense consistency you learned in Chapter 15.
→ Check: Am I writing in my own voice, in the first person ("I"), and keeping my tense consistent throughout?

## 10. TYPES / CLASSIFICATIONS

### Picture-Based Story

Name: Picture-based story
Meaning: A story built from a sequence of pictures, where the writer must invent the character, setting, and connecting details that the pictures only suggest.
Example: Three pictures showing a seed, a small plant, and a tall tree could become a story about a child who plants and cares for a tree over many months.
Contrast: Unlike a story completion, there is no given opening line — the writer builds everything from the images alone.
Quick Check: What must a writer invent for a picture-based story that a story completion already provides? *(the opening idea/first event)*

### Story Completion

Name: Story completion
Meaning: Continuing and finishing a story from a given opening line or paragraph, keeping the same characters, setting, and tone.
Example: Given the opener "The old clock in the hallway struck thirteen, and that was when everything changed," the writer invents what happens next.
Contrast: A picture-based story invents the setting and character freely; a story completion must stay consistent with details already given.
Quick Check: If a story completion begins in the past tense, what tense should the rest of the story usually stay in? *(past tense, for consistency — Chapter 15)*

### Diary Entry

Name: Diary entry
Meaning: A first-person account of real or imagined events from a single day, written in a personal, reflective voice, usually beginning with the date.
Example: "Tuesday, 18 August. Today was the most exciting day of my summer holidays..."
Contrast: Unlike a story with invented characters, a diary entry is usually about the writer's own experiences and feelings, written as "I."
Quick Check: What pronoun does a diary entry usually use throughout? *("I")*

## 11. WORKED EXAMPLE

**Task:** Plan and write a short story completion, continuing this opener: "The old clock in the hallway struck thirteen, and that was when everything changed."

Step 1: Plan the four story elements. Character: a girl named Ira. Setting: her grandmother's old house, at night. Problem/Event: the clock striking thirteen causes something strange to happen. Ending: Ira discovers a hidden door behind the clock.

Step 2: Keep the tense consistent with the opener — it is in the past tense, so the whole story completion should stay in the past tense.

Step 3: Use sequencing words to show the order of events clearly.

Step 4: Add one short piece of dialogue, correctly punctuated.

Answer: "The old clock in the hallway struck thirteen, and that was when everything changed. Ira froze on the stairs, counting the strange chime again in her head. **Slowly**, the tall clock's wooden face swung open like a door. '**Grandma will never believe this,**' she whispered, stepping closer. **Behind** the clock was a narrow passage she had never noticed before, leading down into the dark."

Why: The plan gave the story a clear character, setting, problem, and direction before any sentence was written; the tense stayed consistent throughout; sequencing words guided the reader through the events; and the dialogue was punctuated correctly, as taught in earlier chapters.

## 12. COMPARE AND NOTICE

**Before (list-like):** "A boy found a key. He saw a gate. He opened it. He went in. The end."

**After (planned narrative):** "One rainy afternoon, Arjun found a small brass key half-buried in the garden soil. Curious, he followed the old stone wall until he reached a gate he had never noticed before. Slowly, he pushed the gate open — and gasped."

What changed? The second version names the character, sets the scene, adds a feeling (curiosity), and uses a sequencing word ("Slowly") to build a moment of tension before the ending.
Why? Planning the story elements first turned a bare list of actions into a connected, engaging narrative.

## 16. COMMON CONFUSION

### WATCH OUT!

Common confusion: Learners often write a story as a list of separate actions ("He did this. Then he did that. Then he did this.") without any sense of character, setting, or feeling, or they drift between tenses mid-story because spoken storytelling naturally mixes tenses.

How to check: Ask, "Do I know my character, setting, problem, and ending before I start writing?" and "Have I kept one tense throughout my story, unless I have a clear reason to change it?"

Correct examples: A planned story names its character and setting early and stays in one consistent tense, rather than jumping between "he sees... he saw... he will see" without reason.

## 30. TEACHER SUPPORT NOTE

Likely misconception: Students often write stories as an unconnected list of actions ("He did this, then this, then this") because they begin writing sentence-by-sentence without planning the character, setting, problem, or ending first; tense drift is also common, since spoken storytelling naturally mixes tenses.

Support strategy: Require a brief four-line plan (character/setting/problem/ending) before any story writing begins, and have students read their finished stories aloud, circling every verb to check the tense stays consistent, echoing the strategy already taught in Chapter 15.

Extension: Introduce a simple "show, don't tell" idea for advanced learners — replacing "She was scared" with a small physical detail like "Her hands were shaking" — as a light preview of more advanced descriptive technique.

Challenge: Ask advanced learners to write the same story opener twice, once ending happily and once ending with a twist, and discuss how the planned ending shaped everything that came before it.
