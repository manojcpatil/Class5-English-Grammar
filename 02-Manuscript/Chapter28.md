## 1. PEDAGOGICAL PURPOSE

**Why is this concept being taught here?**
Chapter 27 taught learners to hunt for errors across five categories at once. This chapter takes integration one step further: instead of starting from an error-filled passage, it starts from a **single short reading passage** — the kind of connected text learners meet in real books, notices, and articles — and uses that one passage as the shared source for every kind of task in this book: finding vocabulary in context, identifying grammar at work, transforming sentences, editing, and writing a response. This mirrors how a "language workshop" period works in a real classroom: one shared text, many different things to notice and do with it.

**What should the learner be able to do with it?**
The learner can:
- Read a short passage and locate specific vocabulary, grammar features, and sentence types within it.
- Explain the meaning of an unfamiliar word using context clues from the passage.
- Transform sentences taken directly from the passage (e.g., active to passive, direct to reported, statement to question) without changing their meaning.
- Edit a related passage using the checklist from Chapter 27.
- Write a short piece that responds to, continues, or is modelled on the workshop passage.

| 5 | Edit a related passage | Editing (Ch. 27) |

Rereading the same text for different purposes is a genuine skill — a single passage almost always has more to notice than a single reading reveals.

## 8. GRAMMAR WORD

## Grammar Word: CONTEXT CLUE

A **context clue** is a hint in the surrounding sentence or passage that helps a reader work out the meaning of an unfamiliar word, without needing a dictionary.

### Examples

- "The sky was **crowded** with paper diamonds, dipping and soaring above the streets." — the surrounding words (many kites, a busy sky) suggest "crowded" means "full of many things."
- "Rohan's kite **drifted** silently away." — "silently" and "away" suggest slow, gentle movement, helping explain "drifted."

### Non-examples / Contrast

- Guessing a word's meaning purely from how it *sounds*, without checking the surrounding sentence, often leads to the wrong meaning.

### Quick Check

In the workshop passage, what context clues help you guess the meaning of "soaring"? *(Words like "sky," "dipping," and "above the streets" suggest flying high and smoothly.)*

## 9. CORE EXPLANATION

**Reading for vocabulary in context**
→ Example: "Families climb up before sunrise" — the surrounding words (rooftops, kites, January) help confirm "climb" means going upward, here onto a roof.
→ Contrast: Looking up every unfamiliar word in a dictionary before finishing a sentence interrupts reading; context clues let a reader keep moving and often get close enough to the meaning.
→ Check: Can I guess a sensible meaning for an unfamiliar word just from the words around it?

**Reading for grammar features**
→ Example: "Rohan's kite... **climbed** higher than any other kite" is past tense, matching the story's narrative frame; "his grandfather always **says**" is present tense, showing a habitual, still-true action.
→ Contrast: Not every sentence in a passage will use the same tense — narrative sentences and quoted habitual sayings can differ for a good reason (Chapter 15).
→ Check: Can I name the tense, sentence type, or part of speech being asked about, and point to the exact word in the passage that shows it?

**Transforming sentences from a real passage**
→ Example: Active — "His grandfather taught him how to release the string." → Passive — "He was taught by his grandfather how to release the string."
→ Contrast: A transformation should keep the original meaning; changing "release" to "hold," for instance, would no longer be a fair transformation of the same idea.
→ Check: Does my transformed sentence still mean exactly what the original passage sentence meant?

**Editing within a workshop task**
→ Example: A related passage (not the model passage itself) is deliberately seeded with agreement, tense, punctuation, word-choice, and sentence-construction errors for practice, exactly as in Chapter 27.
→ Contrast: Editing the model passage itself would be pointless, since it contains no errors — workshop editing tasks always use a *related but separate* passage.
→ Check: Am I applying the full five-step checklist, not stopping after the first error I notice?

**Writing a response to the passage**
→ Example: Continuing "The Kite Festival" by imagining where Rohan's lost kite eventually lands, using consistent past tense and at least one piece of correctly punctuated dialogue.
→ Contrast: A response task is not a summary — it should add something new (a continuation, a different character's viewpoint, a diary entry about the same day) rather than simply repeating the passage's own sentences.
→ Check: Does my writing genuinely respond to or extend the passage, using the grammar and vocabulary skills from this book?

## 10. TYPES / CLASSIFICATIONS

### Comprehension Questions

Name: Comprehension questions
Meaning: Questions that check whether the reader understood the passage's events, characters, and ideas.
Example: "Why did Rohan's kite drift away?" "What does his grandfather always tell him?"
Contrast: Comprehension questions are about *what happened*, unlike grammar questions, which are about *how* the passage is written.
Quick Check: Is "What colour was Rohan's kite?" a comprehension question or a grammar question? *(comprehension)*

### Language-Focus Questions

Name: Language-focus questions
Meaning: Questions that direct attention to a specific grammar or vocabulary feature within the passage, rather than to its meaning alone.
Example: "Find one adjective describing the kite." "Find one sentence in the passive voice, or explain why there isn't one."
Contrast: A language-focus question always has a specific, checkable answer pointing to particular words in the text, unlike an open comprehension question that can be answered in the reader's own words.
Quick Check: Is "Find a verb in the past tense" a comprehension question or a language-focus question? *(language-focus)*

## 11. WORKED EXAMPLE

**Task:** Using the workshop passage, (1) explain the meaning of "grinned" using context, and (2) transform the sentence "His grandfather taught him how to release the string" into the passive voice.

Step 1 (Vocabulary): Look at the surrounding words — "He grinned with pride." The context (pride, a moment of success) suggests "grinned" means smiled widely, happily.

Step 2 (Grammar transformation): Identify the doer and receiver. Doer: "his grandfather." Receiver: "him," and the thing taught: "how to release the string."

Step 3: Make the receiver the new subject and add the correct form of "be" plus the past participle.

Answer:
1. "Grinned" means smiled widely and happily, shown by the context clue "with pride."
2. "He was taught by his grandfather how to release the string."

Why: Both answers were found by rereading the same short passage with two different purposes — first for word meaning, then for a specific grammar transformation — exactly as a language workshop asks learners to do.

---

## 12. COMPARE AND NOTICE

**Reading once, for meaning only:** A reader finishes "The Kite Festival" and can say what happened: a boy's kite flew away.

**Reading again, for language:** The same reader now notices that "always says" is present tense (a habit), that "climbed," "grinned," and "drifted" are past tense (the story's events), that "release" could be replaced by "let go" (a synonym already used in the grandfather's own words), and that the sentence about the grandfather's teaching could become passive.

What changed? Nothing in the passage changed — but the *purpose* of reading changed, revealing details a single reading for meaning would miss.
Why? Each rereading, with a specific purpose, uncovers a different layer of the same text — this is the core workshop skill.

## 15. GRAMMAR DETECTIVE

A related passage, written by Leo the parrot, describing a different kite festival:

> "Last year my family goes to the kite festival too. We sees hundreds of kites in the sky! My kites string breaked and it flied away over the rooftops. Everyone were laughing and I is laughing too."

*(Guidance: "goes" → tense/agreement ("went," Ch. 14); "We sees" → agreement ("see," Ch. 13, though tense should also be past: "saw"); "My kites string" → word choice/possession ("My kite's string," Ch. 16); "breaked" → tense, irregular verb ("broke," Ch. 14); "flied" → tense, irregular verb ("flew," Ch. 14); "Everyone were" → agreement ("Everyone was," Ch. 13); "I is" → agreement ("I was," Ch. 13, matching the past-tense story). ## 16. COMMON CONFUSION

### WATCH OUT!

Common confusion: Learners sometimes treat every workshop question as a comprehension question, answering "what happened" even when the task actually asks about a specific grammar or vocabulary feature.

How to check: Ask, "Is this question asking me what the passage *means*, or is it asking me to point to a specific *word or sentence type* in the passage?"

Correct examples: "Why did the kite fly away?" needs an answer about events (comprehension). "Find a verb in the past tense" needs a specific word pointed to in the text (language-focus) — not a retelling of the story.

## 17. LANGUAGE IN REAL LIFE

- **Classroom reading circle:** Students read the same short story together, then take turns answering comprehension, vocabulary, and grammar questions about it.
- **Newspaper study:** A teacher uses one short news article to teach new vocabulary, spot verb tenses, and prompt a short written response, all from the same clipping.
- **Language exam paper:** Many English exams use exactly this workshop structure — one passage, followed by a mixture of comprehension, vocabulary, and grammar questions.
- **Book club discussion:** Readers reread a favourite chapter, this time noticing the author's word choices rather than just the plot.

## 25. SUMMARY

- A **language workshop** returns to the same short passage several times, each time with a different purpose.
- **Context clues** are hints in the surrounding text that help explain an unfamiliar word's meaning.
- **Comprehension questions** ask about meaning and events; **language-focus questions** ask about specific grammar or vocabulary features.
- Sentences from a real passage can be **transformed** (active/passive, direct/reported, statement/question) without changing their meaning.
- Editing tasks use a **related but separate** passage, checked with the five-step checklist from Chapter 27.
- Writing tasks should genuinely **respond to or extend** the passage, not just repeat it.

## 30. TEACHER SUPPORT NOTE

Likely misconception: Students often answer every question about a passage as if it were a comprehension question, even when the task specifically asks for a grammar feature, a transformation, or an edit — because comprehension is the most familiar type of reading question from earlier schooling.

Support strategy: Before setting any workshop task, have students identify aloud what *kind* of question is being asked (comprehension vs. language-focus) before attempting an answer, to build the habit of reading the task itself carefully.

Extension: Introduce longer workshop passages (6-10 sentences) later in the year, once learners are comfortable moving between the six passes on a short passage.

Challenge: Ask advanced learners to write their own six-pass workshop set (comprehension, vocabulary, grammar, transformation, editing companion passage, and writing prompt) around a passage of their own choosing.
