# 1. PEDAGOGICAL PURPOSE

Why is this concept being taught here?
This chapter lays the foundational understanding of what a sentence is, which is crucial for all subsequent grammar learning. Without a clear grasp of complete sentences, learners cannot effectively build more complex structures, understand parts of speech in context, or write coherently. It establishes the basic unit of communication.

What should the learner be able to do with it?
The learner can:
- Identify complete sentences and sentence fragments.
- Construct simple complete sentences with a subject and a predicate.
- Punctuate simple complete sentences correctly with a capital letter and a full stop.
- Understand that sentences express a complete thought.

# 7. DISCOVER / EXPLICIT TEACHING

### Guided Discovery: What makes a complete thought?

Let's look at Rohan's words from the opener: "Woke up. Ate breakfast. Ran to school!"

And Maya's words: "I woke up early. Then I read my favourite book. After that, I walked to school with my brother."

Both Rohan and Maya shared what they did. But Maya's sentences felt more complete, didn't they?

A complete sentence needs two main parts:

1.  **Who or What** the sentence is about (the **Subject**).
2.  **What the Subject does or is** (the **Predicate**).

Think of it like this:

**Subject (Who/What) + Predicate (Does/Is)**

For example:

*   **The sun** (Who/What) **shone brightly** (Does/Is).
*   **Birds** (Who/What) **sang sweet songs** (Does/Is).
*   **Rohan** (Who/What) **watched it** (Does/Is).

# 8. GRAMMAR WORD

## Grammar Word: Sentence

A sentence is a group of words that expresses a complete thought. It always has a subject and a predicate.

### Examples

-   The dog barked loudly.
-   Maya is a good student.
-   They play football every day.

### Non-examples / Contrast

-   Barking loudly. (Missing who/what)
-   A good student. (Missing what she is/does)
-   Play football. (Missing who/what)

### Quick Check

Which of these is a complete sentence?

1.  My friend.
2.  My friend smiled.
3.  Smiled brightly.

# 9. CORE EXPLANATION

A **sentence** is like a mini-story. It tells you who or what did something, or who or what something is.

Every sentence starts with a **capital letter** and ends with a **full stop** (.), a **question mark** (?), or an **exclamation mark** (!). In this chapter, we will focus on sentences that end with a full stop.

**Concept:** A complete sentence has two main parts: a **Subject** and a **Predicate**.

**Example:**

*   **The cat** (Subject - Who/What) **slept soundly** (Predicate - What the cat did).

**Contrast:**

*   "Slept soundly." (Not a complete sentence, because we don't know *who* or *what* slept soundly.)
*   "The cat." (Not a complete sentence, because we don't know *what* the cat did or was.)

**Example:**

*   **Ms. Sharma** (Subject) **teaches grammar** (Predicate).

**Check:**

Does "Teaches grammar" tell you a complete thought? No, it's missing *who* teaches.
Does "Ms. Sharma" tell you a complete thought? No, it's missing *what* Ms. Sharma does.

---

# 10. TYPES / CLASSIFICATIONS

For this chapter, we are focusing on simple declarative sentences (statements).

Name: **Subject**
Meaning: The part of the sentence that tells *who* or *what* the sentence is about.
Example: **Rohan** likes to play. (Rohan is the subject)
Contrast: "Likes to play" is not the subject.
Quick Check: In "The birds sang," what is the subject?

Name: **Predicate**
Meaning: The part of the sentence that tells *what the subject does* or *what the subject is*. It always includes the verb.
Example: Rohan **likes to play**. (Likes to play is the predicate)
Contrast: "Rohan" is not the predicate.
Quick Check: In "The birds sang," what is the predicate?

---

# 11. WORKED EXAMPLE

**Problem:** Is "Walked to school" a complete sentence? If not, how can we make it one?

**Model thinking:**

Step 1: Read the group of words: "Walked to school."

Step 2: Ask: Does it tell me *who* or *what* walked to school? No, it only tells me *what happened*. It's missing the subject.

Step 3: To make it a complete sentence, I need to add a subject. I can add "Maya" or "He" or "The students."

Answer: No, "Walked to school" is not a complete sentence.

Why: It is missing a subject (who or what performed the action).

Corrected example: **Maya** walked to school.

# 16. COMMON CONFUSION

### WATCH OUT!

Common confusion: Thinking that any group of words with a verb is a sentence.

How to check: Always ask yourself: "Who or what is doing the action?" If you can't find a clear 'who' or 'what', it's probably a fragment.

Correct examples:
-   **She** is singing. (She is doing the singing)
-   **The flowers** bloomed. (The flowers bloomed)

# 25. SUMMARY

-   A sentence is a group of words that expresses a complete thought.
-   Every complete sentence has two main parts: a Subject (who or what) and a Predicate (what the subject does or is).
-   Sentences begin with a capital letter and end with a full stop (.), question mark (?), or exclamation mark (!).
-   Sentence fragments are incomplete thoughts and are missing a subject or a predicate.

---

# 26. KEY TERMS

| Term | Simple Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sentence | A group of words that tells a complete idea. |
| Subject | The part of a sentence that tells who or what the sentence is about. |
| Predicate | The part of a sentence that tells what the subject does or is. |
| Fragment | A group of words that is not a complete sentence. |
| Capital Letter | The large letter used at the start of a sentence. |
| Full Stop | The dot (.) used at the end of a sentence. |

# 30. TEACHER SUPPORT NOTE

**Likely misconception:** Students may confuse phrases with sentences, especially if the phrase contains a verb (e.g., "running quickly"). Emphasise the "who/what" aspect of the subject.

**Support strategy:** Use colour-coding for subject and predicate. Provide sentence strips that students can physically manipulate to form complete sentences.

**Extension:** Introduce simple compound subjects (e.g., "Maya and Rohan").

**Challenge:** Ask students to identify the subject and predicate in slightly longer, but still simple, sentences.
