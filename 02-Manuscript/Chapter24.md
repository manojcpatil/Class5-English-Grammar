## 1. PEDAGOGICAL PURPOSE

**Why is this concept being taught here?**
Learners have already built accuracy at the word and sentence level (Units 2-5), expanded their vocabulary (Unit 6), and learned to craft skilful, connected sentences (Unit 7). This chapter takes the next natural step: organising several connected sentences into a single, purposeful **paragraph**. It also introduces descriptive writing, teaching learners to choose vivid, specific, sensory detail rather than vague description — a skill that will support every writing task in the chapters that follow (letters, notices, and creative composition).

**What should the learner be able to do with it?**
The learner can:
- Identify the topic sentence of a paragraph.
- Write a clear topic sentence for a given subject.
- Add supporting details that relate directly to the topic sentence.
- Arrange sentences within a paragraph in a logical order.
- Use sensory and specific descriptive detail instead of vague description.
- Edit a paragraph for unity (removing sentences that do not belong) and order.

## 7. DISCOVER / EXPLICIT TEACHING

### Explicit explanation

A **paragraph** is a group of sentences that work together to develop **one main idea**. A well-built paragraph usually has:

| Part | Job |
|---|---|
| Topic sentence | States the main idea of the paragraph, usually first |
| Supporting details | Give more information, examples, or description about the main idea |
| Logical order | Arranges the sentences so ideas flow sensibly from one to the next |
| Unity | Every sentence relates to the topic sentence — nothing wanders off-topic |

**Descriptive writing** adds a further layer: instead of vague words, it uses **sensory details** — what something looks, sounds, smells, feels, and (sometimes) tastes like — to help the reader picture the scene clearly.

---

## 8. GRAMMAR WORD

## Grammar Word: TOPIC SENTENCE

A **topic sentence** is the sentence, usually the first in a paragraph, that clearly states the main idea the rest of the paragraph will develop.

### Examples

- "The school garden is a peaceful corner behind the main building." (introduces the topic: the garden, and its mood — peaceful)
- "Monday mornings at our house are always chaotic." (introduces the topic: Monday mornings, and the mood — chaotic)

### Non-examples / Contrast

- "The garden has flowers." (too plain and narrow — it does not set up a fuller main idea for the paragraph to develop)
- A paragraph with no topic sentence at all, where the reader must guess the main idea from a jumble of details.

### Quick Check

Which sentence would make a better topic sentence: "My dog is brown," or "My dog is the most playful member of our family"? *(The second — it sets up a main idea the rest of the paragraph can develop.)*

---

## 9. CORE EXPLANATION

**The topic sentence sets the direction**
→ Example: "Our classroom becomes a lively, colourful place every December." (topic: classroom in December)
→ Contrast: A paragraph that begins with an unrelated fact ("Yesterday I ate breakfast.") gives the reader no clue what the paragraph is really about.
→ Check: If I read only the first sentence, would I know what the whole paragraph is going to describe or explain?

**Supporting details develop the idea**
→ Example: After "Our classroom becomes a lively, colourful place every December," add details — paper snowflakes on the windows, a small tree in the corner, cheerful carols during break.
→ Contrast: A detail like "My pencil box is blue" does not support this topic sentence at all — it belongs in a different paragraph.
→ Check: Does each new sentence add something that helps explain or picture the topic sentence?

**Logical order guides the reader**
→ Example (spatial order): Describing a room from the door, to the walls, to the far corner.
→ Example (time order): Describing a morning routine from waking up to leaving for school.
→ Contrast: Jumping between "the bench in the shade" and then back to "the path near the gate" and then back to "the bench" again feels confusing and repetitive.
→ Check: Does one sentence lead naturally into the next — by space, by time, or by importance?

**Sensory detail creates vivid description**
→ Example: Instead of "The bakery smelled nice," write "The bakery smelled of warm, buttery bread fresh from the oven."
→ Contrast: Vague words like *nice, good, nice-looking* tell the reader almost nothing specific.
→ Check: Have I used a specific sight, sound, smell, touch, or taste detail instead of a vague word?

## 10. TYPES / CLASSIFICATIONS

### Ways to Order a Descriptive Paragraph

Name: Spatial order
Meaning: Describing details in the order they appear in space (e.g., top to bottom, near to far, left to right).
Example: "At the front of the classroom stands the teacher's desk. Behind it, a large green board covers the wall. Rows of wooden desks fill the rest of the room."
Contrast: Time order instead describes events as they happen one after another.
Quick Check: Which order would suit describing a tall tree from its roots to its top branches? *(spatial order)*

Name: Time order (chronological order)
Meaning: Describing details in the order they happened.
Example: "First, dark clouds gathered. Then, the wind picked up. Finally, the rain began to fall."
Contrast: Spatial order instead moves through a scene by location, not by time.
Quick Check: Which order would suit describing how a caterpillar becomes a butterfly? *(time order)*

### The Five Senses for Description

Name: Sensory details
Meaning: Specific details about how something looks, sounds, smells, feels, or tastes.
Example: sight — "golden sunlight"; sound — "the clatter of pots"; smell — "the sharp scent of chalk dust"; touch — "the rough bark of the tree"; taste — "the tangy sourness of a lemon"
Contrast: A description with only one sense used throughout (always sight) is flatter than one that mixes senses.
Quick Check: Which sense does "the crunch of dry leaves underfoot" appeal to? *(sound, and touch)*

## 11. WORKED EXAMPLE

**Task:** Write a short descriptive paragraph about a rainy day, using a topic sentence, at least three supporting details, and sensory language.

Step 1: Choose a clear topic sentence that sets the mood. *"The first rain of the season transformed our street in a matter of minutes."*

Step 2: Brainstorm sensory details — sight (dark clouds, puddles forming), sound (drumming on the roof, children laughing), smell (wet earth), touch (cool droplets on skin).

Step 3: Arrange the details in a logical order — here, time order works well, since the rain changes the street as it falls.

Step 4: Write the full paragraph, keeping every sentence connected to the topic.

Answer: "The first rain of the season transformed our street in a matter of minutes. Thick grey clouds rolled in, and within moments, fat drops began to drum steadily on our tin roof. The dry earth outside gave off a fresh, earthy smell as it soaked up the water. Children ran out barefoot, laughing and splashing through the puddles that had already begun to form along the road."

Why: The topic sentence sets up the idea (the rain transforming the street), each supporting detail adds a different sensory picture, and the details are arranged in the order they would actually happen.

## 12. COMPARE AND NOTICE

**Before (vague, no clear order):** "The market was busy. There were vegetables. I saw a boy. It was noisy. My mother bought onions. There was a dog."

**After (topic sentence, ordered, sensory):** "The morning market buzzed with activity. Vendors called out the prices of bright red tomatoes and leafy green spinach, stacked in neat piles. A small dog darted between the stalls, chasing a scrap of bread. Amid the noise, my mother calmly haggled over a bag of onions, her basket slowly filling with the week's vegetables."

What changed? A clear topic sentence was added, the details were arranged so the scene builds naturally, and vague words like "busy" and "noisy" were replaced with specific sights and sounds.
Why? The improved version lets the reader picture and almost hear the market, instead of just being told a list of facts about it.

## 15. GRAMMAR DETECTIVE

Student paragraph:

> "My birthday party was fun. We had a cake. My uncle fixed his bicycle yesterday. There were balloons everywhere. I got a new book as a present."

Questions:
1. What is wrong with this paragraph?
2. Which sentence should be removed, and why?
3. How could the paragraph be improved with more descriptive detail?

*(Guidance: The sentence about the uncle's bicycle does not belong — it breaks the unity of the paragraph. The remaining sentences could also be made more vivid: what colour were the balloons, what flavour was the cake, what was the new book about?)*

## 16. COMMON CONFUSION

### WATCH OUT!

Common confusion: Learners often write a list of unconnected facts (a "story in bullet points") rather than a flowing paragraph, or they include one or two sentences that do not relate to the topic sentence at all.

How to check: Ask, "Does every sentence in my paragraph relate back to my topic sentence? Does one sentence lead naturally into the next?"

Correct examples: A paragraph about a rainy day should not suddenly mention what someone had for breakfast three days earlier — that breaks unity, even if the sentence itself is grammatically correct.

## 25. SUMMARY

- A **paragraph** is a group of sentences that develop one main idea.
- The **topic sentence** usually comes first and states the main idea clearly.
- **Supporting details** add information, examples, or description that relate directly to the topic sentence.
- Details should be arranged in a **logical order** — often spatial order or time order.
- **Sensory details** (sight, sound, smell, touch, taste) make descriptive writing vivid and specific.
- A well-built paragraph has **unity** — every sentence belongs to the same main idea.

---

## 26. KEY TERMS

| Term | Simple Meaning |
|---|---|
| Paragraph | A group of sentences developing one main idea |
| Topic sentence | The sentence that states the paragraph's main idea |
| Supporting detail | A sentence that develops or explains the topic sentence |
| Spatial order | Arranging details by location in space |
| Time order (chronological order) | Arranging details in the order they happened |
| Sensory detail | A specific detail about sight, sound, smell, touch, or taste |
| Unity | Every sentence in a paragraph relating to the same main idea |

## 30. TEACHER SUPPORT NOTE

Likely misconception: Students often write a list of separate facts rather than a connected paragraph, and may include off-topic sentences because they wrote them as they thought of them, without checking unity afterwards.

Support strategy: Teach a simple "one paragraph, one idea" check — after drafting, students underline the topic sentence and then check whether every other sentence connects back to it, crossing out or moving anything that does not.

Extension: Introduce order of importance (least to most important detail, or most to least) as a third possible ordering strategy for more advanced learners.

Challenge: Ask advanced learners to write the same descriptive paragraph twice — once in spatial order and once in time order — and discuss how the effect changes.
