## 1. PEDAGOGICAL PURPOSE

**Why is this concept being taught here?**
Chapter 14 taught the three basic tenses in isolation. Real writing, however, uses tense across whole passages, and one of the most common errors in young writers' work is switching tense without reason mid-paragraph. This chapter also introduces the present continuous form ("is/are + -ing"), which learners need to describe ongoing action, and teaches them to keep tense steady and appropriate to the purpose of a piece of writing.

**What should the learner be able to do with it?**
The learner can:
- Use the present tense for routines and facts.
- Use the past tense to tell a story that has already happened.
- Use "will" or "going to" for plans and predictions.
- Recognise and form the present continuous for actions happening right now.
- Keep tense consistent across a paragraph and correct unnecessary shifts.

## 7. DISCOVER / EXPLICIT TEACHING

### Explicit explanation

When we write more than one sentence, we usually keep the **same tense** throughout, unless the meaning truly changes in time. This is called **tense consistency**.

- Writing about a **routine or fact** → stay in the **present tense**.
- Telling a **story about the past** → stay in the **past tense**.
- Describing **plans or predictions** → use **"will"** or **"going to"**.
- Describing something happening **right now** → use the **present continuous** ("is/are" + verb-**ing**).

---

## 8. GRAMMAR WORD

## Grammar Word: TENSE CONSISTENCY

**Tense consistency** means using the same tense throughout a piece of writing (or a clearly marked section of it) so that the timeline stays clear for the reader.

### Examples

- Consistent past: "Leo flew to the window. He landed on the perch. He looked outside."
- Consistent present: "Leo flies to the window every morning. He lands on the perch. He looks outside."

### Non-examples / Contrast

- Inconsistent: "Leo flew to the window. He lands on the perch. He will look outside." (past → present → future, with no reason for the change)

### Quick Check

Is this consistent? "Maya reads a book. She turned the page." *(No — it shifts from present to past without reason.)*

---

## 9. CORE EXPLANATION

**Routines and facts → present tense**
→ Example: "Every morning, the sun **rises** in the east."
→ Contrast: Do not slip into past tense mid-routine: "The sun rises... and it set." (incorrect shift)
→ Check: Is this something that happens regularly or is always true? Use present tense throughout.

**Past narratives → past tense**
→ Example: "Yesterday, Rohan **woke** up early. He **brushed** his teeth and **ate** breakfast."
→ Contrast: Avoid switching to present mid-story: "He woke up... and he eats breakfast." (incorrect shift)
→ Check: Has the whole story already finished? Keep every verb in the past.

**Plans and predictions → "will" or "going to"**
→ Example: "Next week, we **will visit** the museum." / "We **are going to visit** the museum."
→ Contrast: "Will" often expresses a decision made now or a general prediction; "going to" often expresses a plan already decided. Both are acceptable at this level.
→ Check: Is the action still in the future? Use "will" or "going to" consistently.

**Happening right now → present continuous**
→ Example: "Look! Leo **is flying** around the room right now."
→ Contrast with simple present: "Leo **flies** around the room every day" (habit) vs. "Leo **is flying** around the room right now" (happening at this moment).
→ Check: Is the action in progress at this very moment? Use "is/am/are" + verb-**ing**.

---

## 10. TYPES / CLASSIFICATIONS

### Present Continuous — Formation

Name: Present continuous
Meaning: Formed with **am/is/are** + the base verb + **-ing**, used for actions happening right now or temporarily.
Example: I **am reading**. She **is writing**. They **are playing**.
Contrast: Simple present ("I read every day") describes a habit; present continuous ("I am reading now") describes the action in progress.
Quick Check: Complete: "Right now, Rohan ___ (play) football." *(is playing)*

### Will vs. Going To

Name: Future forms
Meaning: Both express future time; "will" often for quick decisions or predictions, "going to" often for plans already made.
Example: "I think it **will rain**." (prediction) vs. "We **are going to visit** Grandma this weekend." (plan)
Contrast: At this level, both are acceptable for general future meaning; the distinction is introduced gently, not tested strictly.
Quick Check: Which sounds more like a plan already decided: "I will go" or "I am going to go"?

## 11. WORKED EXAMPLE

**Passage to fix:** "Every day, Maya feeds her fish. Yesterday, she cleans the tank and she will change the water."

Step 1: Identify the intended time frame. The first sentence ("Every day") signals a routine — present tense.

Step 2: Check the second sentence. "Yesterday" signals the past, but the verb "cleans" is present tense — this is an error.

Step 3: Check the third sentence. "She will change" is future, but there is no future time signal — likely meant to continue the past story.

Answer: "Every day, Maya feeds her fish. Yesterday, she **cleaned** the tank and she **changed** the water."

Why: The first sentence stays present because it describes a routine; the second and third sentences must both be past because "yesterday" sets the whole story in the past.

---

## 12. COMPARE AND NOTICE

**Incorrect:** "Right now, Leo eats his seeds and he was singing."
**Correct:** "Right now, Leo **is eating** his seeds and he **is singing**."

What changed? Both verbs became present continuous.
Why? "Right now" signals an action in progress at this very moment, which needs the present continuous form, not simple present or past.

## 16. COMMON CONFUSION

### WATCH OUT!

Common confusion: Learners often mix simple present ("I play") with present continuous ("I am playing") without noticing the difference in meaning, or they drift between tenses mid-paragraph without a time signal to justify the change.

How to check: Ask, "Is this a habit (simple present) or something happening right now (present continuous)?" and "Did my time frame change, or did I just drift?"

Correct examples: "I play football every day" (habit) vs. "I am playing football right now" (in progress).

## 30. TEACHER SUPPORT NOTE

Likely misconception: Students often drift tense mid-paragraph because spoken storytelling naturally mixes tenses; they may not notice the shift in their own writing.

Support strategy: Have students read their own paragraphs aloud and circle every verb, checking that all circled verbs match the intended time frame.

Extension: Introduce "going to" versus "will" as a light discussion point (decided plan vs. spontaneous decision), without formal testing.

Challenge: Ask advanced learners to identify the "historic present" in a simple story excerpt and discuss its effect.
