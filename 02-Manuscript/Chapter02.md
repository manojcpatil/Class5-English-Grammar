# 1. PEDAGOGICAL PURPOSE

Why is this concept being taught here?
This chapter builds on the understanding of complete sentences by introducing how sentences can be used for different purposes. It helps learners communicate more effectively and understand the intentions behind different types of spoken and written language. It also reinforces the importance of correct punctuation.

What should the learner be able to do with it?
The learner can:
- Identify statements, questions, commands, and exclamations.
- Construct simple sentences of each kind.
- Punctuate each kind of sentence correctly.
- Understand the purpose or intention behind each kind of sentence.

# 8. GRAMMAR WORD

## Grammar Word: Kinds of Sentences

Sentences can be grouped into different kinds based on their purpose or what they do. The four main kinds are Statements, Questions, Commands, and Exclamations.

### Examples

-   **Statement:** The sun is shining.
-   **Question:** Is the sun shining?
-   **Command:** Look at the sun.
-   **Exclamation:** What a bright sun!

### Non-examples / Contrast

-   "The sun." (Not a sentence)
-   "Shining brightly." (Not a complete sentence)
-   "Sun is shining?" (Missing capital letter, wrong word order for a question)

### Quick Check

What kind of sentence is this: "The birds are singing."?

# 9. CORE EXPLANATION

Every time we speak or write, we use sentences to do different jobs. There are four main kinds of sentences:

1.  **Statements (Declarative Sentences):**
    *   **Purpose:** To tell you something or give information.
    *   **Punctuation:** Ends with a full stop (.).
    *   **Example:** Maya loves to read books.

2.  **Questions (Interrogative Sentences):**
    *   **Purpose:** To ask something.
    *   **Punctuation:** Ends with a question mark (?).
    *   **Example:** Does Rohan play football?

3.  **Commands (Imperative Sentences):**
    *   **Purpose:** To give an order, make a request, or give an instruction. The subject (you) is often understood but not stated.
    *   **Punctuation:** Ends with a full stop (.) or sometimes an exclamation mark (!) if the command is strong.
    *   **Example:** Please close the door. (Request)
    *   **Example:** Close the door! (Strong command)

4.  **Exclamations (Exclamatory Sentences):**
    *   **Purpose:** To show strong feelings like surprise, excitement, anger, or joy.
    *   **Punctuation:** Ends with an exclamation mark (!).
    *   **Example:** What a beautiful day it is!

# 10. TYPES / CLASSIFICATIONS

Name: **Statement** (Declarative Sentence)
Meaning: A sentence that tells or declares something.
Example: Ms. Sharma teaches English.
Contrast: "Does Ms. Sharma teach English?" (This is a question).
Quick Check: What punctuation mark does a statement end with?

Name: **Question** (Interrogative Sentence)
Meaning: A sentence that asks something.
Example: Where is Leo the parrot?
Contrast: "Leo the parrot is here." (This is a statement).
Quick Check: What punctuation mark does a question end with?

Name: **Command** (Imperative Sentence)
Meaning: A sentence that gives an order, instruction, or makes a request. The subject 'you' is usually hidden.
Example: Read the next chapter. (You read the next chapter.)
Contrast: "You will read the next chapter." (This is a statement).
Quick Check: Can a command end with a full stop or an exclamation mark?

Name: **Exclamation** (Exclamatory Sentence)
Meaning: A sentence that shows strong feeling or emotion.
Example: What a fantastic idea!
Contrast: "That is a fantastic idea." (This is a statement).
Quick Check: What punctuation mark does an exclamation end with?

# 11. WORKED EXAMPLE

**Problem:** Change the statement "Rohan is a good student." into a question, a command, and an exclamation.

**Model thinking:**

Step 1: Original sentence: "Rohan is a good student." (Statement)

Step 2: To make a **question**, I need to ask something. I can change the word order or add a question word.
    *   *Thinking:* "Is Rohan a good student?" (Changed word order)

Step 3: To make a **command**, I need to tell someone to do something. This sentence doesn't easily become a command about Rohan being a student. I might need to change the verb or focus.
    *   *Thinking:* "Be a good student, Rohan." (This is a command to Rohan.)

Step 4: To make an **exclamation**, I need to show strong feeling.
    *   *Thinking:* "What a good student Rohan is!" or "Rohan is such a good student!"

Answer:
*   **Question:** Is Rohan a good student?
*   **Command:** Be a good student, Rohan.
*   **Exclamation:** What a good student Rohan is!

# 16. COMMON CONFUSION

### WATCH OUT!

Common confusion: Mixing up questions and exclamations, or commands and statements.

How to check:
-   **Questions** always ask for information and usually have a different word order (e.g., verb before subject).
-   **Exclamations** show strong feeling and often start with "What a..." or "How...".
-   **Commands** tell someone to do something, and the subject "you" is usually not written.

Correct examples:
-   **Statement:** You are very kind.
-   **Question:** Are you very kind?
-   **Exclamation:** How kind you are!
-   **Command:** Be kind.
