## 1. PEDAGOGICAL PURPOSE

**Why is this concept being taught here?**
Learners have now built a strong grammatical foundation and can construct accurate sentences of many kinds. This chapter turns that knowledge outward, into real spoken communication — the everyday language of greeting people, making requests, asking questions, responding politely, and asking for clarification when something is unclear. It is less about a single grammar rule and more about using the grammar already learned appropriately and politely.

**What should the learner be able to do with it?**
The learner can:
- Use appropriate greetings for different times of day and situations.
- Make polite requests using suitable phrases.
- Ask questions to get information or clarification.
- Respond appropriately to greetings, requests, and questions.
- Take part in a short role play using natural, polite classroom English.

Everyday spoken English uses certain useful phrases for common purposes:

| Purpose | Useful Phrases |
|---|---|
| Greeting | "Good morning/afternoon/evening," "Hello," "Hi, how are you?" |
| Making a polite request | "Could you please...", "May I...", "Would you mind..." |
| Asking a question | "What...", "Where...", "Why...", "Can you tell me...?" |
| Responding politely | "Yes, of course," "I'm sorry, but...", "Thank you," "You're welcome." |
| Asking for clarification | "Sorry, could you repeat that?", "What do you mean?", "Could you explain that again?" |

## 8. GRAMMAR WORD

## Grammar Word: REGISTER

**Register** means choosing words and a tone that suit the situation and the person you are speaking to — for example, speaking more formally to a stranger or a teacher, and more casually to a close friend.

### Examples

- To a teacher: "Excuse me, may I ask a question?"
- To a close friend: "Hey, quick question!"

### Non-examples / Contrast

- Using very casual language with a shopkeeper you have never met ("Oi, give me that!") does not match the situation and can sound rude, even if it is not meant that way.

### Quick Check

Which sounds more appropriate when speaking to your school principal: "What's up?" or "Good morning, may I speak with you?"

## 9. CORE EXPLANATION

**Greeting appropriately**
→ Example: "Good morning, Ms. Sharma." (formal, to a teacher)
→ Contrast: "Hi!" (casual, to a friend)
→ Check: Does my greeting match the time of day and the person I am speaking to?

**Making polite requests**
→ Example: "Could you please pass the ruler?"
→ Contrast: "Give me the ruler." (blunt, less polite)
→ Check: Have I used a polite phrase like "could," "please," or "may I," instead of only a command?

**Asking for clarification**
→ Example: "Sorry, could you say that again, please?"
→ Contrast: Staying silent and guessing what was said can lead to misunderstandings.
→ Check: If I did not understand something, have I asked politely instead of guessing?

**Responding appropriately**
→ Example: "Thank you for asking! I'm doing well." (response to a greeting)
→ Contrast: Ignoring a greeting or question is impolite, even if unintentional.
→ Check: Have I responded to what the other person actually said or asked?

## 10. TYPES / CLASSIFICATIONS

### Greetings by Time of Day

Name: Time-based greetings
Meaning: Different greetings suit different times of day.
Example: "Good morning" (before noon), "Good afternoon" (noon to evening), "Good evening" (evening onward)
Contrast: "Good night" is used only when parting or going to bed, not as a greeting on meeting someone.
Quick Check: Which greeting would you use at 4 PM? *("Good afternoon")*

### Polite Request Phrases

Name: Request starters
Meaning: Phrases that soften a request and make it sound polite rather than like an order.
Example: "Could you...", "Would you mind...", "May I..."
Contrast: A direct command ("Close the door.") is not impolite in every situation, but adding a request starter often makes it sound more courteous ("Could you close the door, please?").
Quick Check: Which sounds more polite: "Open the window" or "Could you open the window, please?"

## 11. WORKED EXAMPLE

**Situation:** You want to ask your teacher if you can leave the classroom to get some water.

Step 1: Think about who you are speaking to — a teacher, so a polite, slightly formal register is appropriate.

Step 2: Choose a polite request phrase — "May I..." or "Could I..." both work well for asking permission.

Step 3: State the request clearly and add "please" for extra politeness.

Answer: "**Excuse me, may I please go and get some water?**"

Why: "Excuse me" politely gets the teacher's attention, "may I" is an appropriate way to ask permission from an adult, and "please" adds courtesy.

## 12. COMPARE AND NOTICE

**Less polite:** "I don't get it. Say it again."
**More polite:** "I'm sorry, I don't quite understand. Could you please explain that again?"

What changed? The blunt statement was replaced with an apology, a softer way of admitting confusion, and a polite request phrase.
Why? The more polite version is just as clear, but it is more likely to be well received and shows respect for the listener.

## 16. COMMON CONFUSION

### WATCH OUT!

Common confusion: Learners sometimes think that being polite means using very long or complicated sentences, when in fact even a short phrase can be polite if it includes the right words ("please," "could," "may I").

How to check: Ask, "Have I included at least one polite word or phrase, even if my sentence is short?"

Correct examples: "Could I have a pencil, please?" is both short and polite — length is not what makes a sentence courteous.

## 30. TEACHER SUPPORT NOTE

Likely misconception: Students may believe politeness only matters in formal writing, not in spoken conversation, or may over-formalise their speech with friends when a casual register is perfectly appropriate.

Support strategy: Use paired role play extensively, and discuss as a class how the same message can be delivered in different registers depending on the listener.

Extension: Introduce telephone etiquette as a specific real-life register (formal greeting, clear identification, polite closing).

Challenge: Ask advanced learners to write the same short conversation twice — once for a close friend and once for a stranger — and discuss what changed.
