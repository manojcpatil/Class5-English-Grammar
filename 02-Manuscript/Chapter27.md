## 1. PEDAGOGICAL PURPOSE

**Why is this concept being taught here?**
Every chapter from 13 onward has included its own "Grammar Detective" activity, each focused on a single kind of error. Learners have now met nearly every grammar concept the book teaches, so this chapter's purpose is different from every chapter before it: it does not introduce new grammar. Instead, it **integrates** everything already learned — agreement, tense, punctuation, word choice, and sentence construction — into single passages containing several different kinds of error at once, and gives learners a repeatable checklist for finding them all, not just the one they happen to notice first.

**What should the learner be able to do with it?**
The learner can:
- Recall the five broad categories of error covered across the book (agreement, tense, punctuation, word choice, sentence construction).
- Use a five-step self-editing checklist to check a passage systematically, rather than only fixing the first error noticed.
- Identify multiple different kinds of error within the same passage.
- Explain *why* each error is wrong, using the correct grammar term.
- Correct a passage fully, checking every category before considering it finished.

## 7. DISCOVER / EXPLICIT TEACHING

### Explicit explanation

A real editor does not check a piece of writing only once, looking for only one thing. Instead, they work through a **checklist**, checking for one category of error at a time, all the way through the passage, before moving to the next category:

| Step | Check | Ask Yourself |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Agreement | Does every verb match its subject in number? (Chapter 13) | Singular subject → singular verb? Plural subject → plural verb? |
| 2. Tense | Is the tense correct and consistent throughout? (Chapters 14-15) | Does every verb match the intended time, without unexplained shifts? |
| 3. Punctuation | Are capitals, end marks, commas, apostrophes, and quotation marks all correct? (Chapter 16) | Does every sentence start and end correctly, and is direct speech marked? |
| 4. Word choice | Is every word spelled and chosen correctly for its meaning? (Chapters 18-19) | Right homophone? No vague or repeated words? |
| 5. Sentence construction | Is each sentence complete, well-ordered, and not needlessly choppy? (Chapter 20) | Complete thought? Natural word order? No unnecessary repetition? |

## 8. GRAMMAR WORD

## Grammar Word: SELF-EDITING CHECKLIST

A **self-editing checklist** is a fixed list of categories a writer checks, one at a time, all the way through a piece of writing, to catch as many different kinds of error as possible — rather than reading once and fixing only whatever is noticed first.

### Examples

- Checking a whole paragraph for agreement errors first, then going back through the same paragraph again just for tense, then again just for punctuation.

### Non-examples / Contrast

- Reading a passage once and fixing only the first mistake spotted, then assuming the rest must be correct, is not using a checklist — it usually misses errors from categories the writer wasn't actively looking for.

### Quick Check

Why might checking a passage five separate times (once per category) catch more errors than checking it only once? *(Because it is hard to notice every kind of error at the same time; focusing on one category at a time makes each pass more thorough.)*

## 9. CORE EXPLANATION

**Agreement errors (Chapter 13)**
→ Example: "The basket of apples **are** heavy." → should be **is** (true subject: basket, singular).
→ Contrast: Not every agreement error involves an interrupting phrase — some are as simple as "The dogs **barks**." → should be **bark**.
→ Check: Have I found the *true* subject of each verb, ignoring any interrupting phrase, and matched the verb to it?

**Tense errors (Chapters 14-15)**
→ Example: "Yesterday, we **go** to the market." → should be **went**.
→ Contrast: Some tense errors are about the wrong *form* ("goed" instead of "went"); others are about *consistency* — correct forms used in the wrong order ("He woke up... and he eats breakfast.").
→ Check: Does every verb match the intended time frame, and have I avoided any unexplained shift in tense?

**Punctuation errors (Chapter 16)**
→ Example: "wheres my pencil box" → should be "**W**here's my pencil box**?**"
→ Contrast: Punctuation errors include missing capitals, missing end marks, missing apostrophes, and missing quotation marks around direct speech — check each separately.
→ Check: Does every sentence begin with a capital letter, end with the correct mark, and show ownership/contraction and direct speech correctly?

**Word choice errors (Chapters 18-19)**
→ Example: "**Its** raining today." → should be **It's** (it is).
→ Contrast: Word choice errors also include vague, overused words ("nice," "good," "thing") that could be replaced with something more precise, as taught in Chapter 17 and reinforced in Chapter 20.
→ Check: Is every homophone spelled correctly for its meaning, and is every word the most precise choice available?

**Sentence construction errors (Chapter 20)**
→ Example: "The dog. It barked. It ran." (choppy, could be combined) → "The dog barked and ran."
→ Contrast: A sentence construction error can also be an incomplete sentence ("Because it was raining.") with no main clause to complete the thought.
→ Check: Is every sentence complete, in natural word order, and free of unnecessary repetition or choppiness?

## 10. TYPES / CLASSIFICATIONS

### Single-Category vs. Multi-Category Passages

Name: Single-category and multi-category error passages
Meaning: A single-category passage contains only one kind of error (as in earlier chapters' Grammar Detective sections); a multi-category passage, like the ones in this chapter, mixes several kinds of error together, closer to real writing.
Example: Chapter 13's Grammar Detective only tested agreement; this chapter's passages test agreement, tense, punctuation, word choice, and sentence construction together.
Contrast: Multi-category passages are harder because fixing one error can distract attention from another nearby error of a different kind.
Quick Check: Why might a multi-category passage be closer to real-life editing than a single-category one? *(Real writing usually contains a mixture of different kinds of error, not just one kind at a time.)*

### Error vs. Style

Name: Genuine error vs. stylistic choice
Meaning: A genuine error breaks a grammar rule; a stylistic choice is a valid option a writer selects on purpose (for example, choosing "will" over "going to," or choosing a short sentence for effect).
Example: "The team **is** practising" (standard) vs. "The team **are** arguing among themselves" (a deliberate, less common but valid choice in some varieties of English, as noted in Chapter 13) — this is a style choice, not an error.
Contrast: "The team **be** practising" is not a valid stylistic option at this level — it breaks the standard agreement rule altogether.
Quick Check: Is choosing a short, punchy sentence instead of combining it with a longer one always an error? *(No — sometimes it's a deliberate stylistic choice, as discussed in Chapter 20's Think Deeper.)*

## 11. WORKED EXAMPLE

**Passage to edit:** "My sister and me goes to the park every Sunday. We plays cricket and eat lunch. Last week it rain and we gets wet. Its very funny now when we remember it."

Step 1 (Agreement): Check each subject-verb pair. "My sister and me goes" → the subject "My sister and I" is plural, so it should be "go," not "goes." Also, "and me" should be "and I" as the subject of the sentence.

Step 2 (Tense): "Last week it rain" signals the past but uses the base form — should be "rained." "We gets wet" should be "got wet" to match the past-tense story.

Step 3 (Punctuation): "Its very funny" should be "**It's** very funny" (it is).

Step 4 (Word choice): No homophone or vague-word errors remain once the apostrophe above is fixed.

Step 5 (Sentence construction): Each sentence is already complete and in natural order, so no further changes are needed here.

Answer: "My sister and I **go** to the park every Sunday. We **play** cricket and eat lunch. Last week it **rained** and we **got** wet. **It's** very funny now when we remember it."

Why: Working through the checklist one category at a time caught errors from three different categories (agreement, tense, and punctuation) that might have been missed if the passage had only been read once, looking for just one kind of mistake.

---

## 12. COMPARE AND NOTICE

**Before (uncorrected):** "The class of students was excited. They was going on a trip tomorrow, and there bags was already packed. Its going to be a great day!"

**After (fully corrected):** "The class of students **was** excited. **They were** going on a trip tomorrow, and **their** bags **were** already packed. **It's** going to be a great day!"

What changed? "was" (correct for "class") stayed the same; "they was" became "they were" (agreement); "there bags" became "their bags" (word choice/homophone); "bags was" became "bags were" (agreement); "Its" became "It's" (word choice/apostrophe).
Why? Checking the passage category by category caught errors from both the agreement and word-choice groups, which sit right next to each other in the same short passage.

## 16. COMMON CONFUSION

### WATCH OUT!

Common confusion: Learners often fix the *first* error they notice and then stop checking, assuming the rest of the passage must be correct — especially when the first error they spot happens to match the skill from the most recently studied chapter.

How to check: Ask, "Have I gone through the whole checklist — agreement, tense, punctuation, word choice, sentence construction — or did I stop after the first thing I noticed?"

Correct examples: A thorough editor rereads the same short passage five times, once per checklist category, rather than once overall.

## 17. LANGUAGE IN REAL LIFE

- **Checking your own homework:** Reading a written answer once for spelling, once for punctuation, and once for sense, rather than only once overall.
- **Peer editing in class:** Two students swap paragraphs and each uses the checklist to find errors the writer may have missed in their own work.
- **A published book's editor:** Real books go through several separate rounds of editing (content, then grammar, then punctuation, then final proofreading) before printing — very similar to this chapter's step-by-step checklist.
- **Checking a message before sending:** Quickly rereading a text message for a missing word or wrong homophone before pressing send.

## 25. SUMMARY

- A **self-editing checklist** helps a writer check a passage systematically, one category at a time, rather than only once.
- The five checklist steps are: **agreement**, **tense**, **punctuation**, **word choice**, and **sentence construction**.
- Multi-category passages, closer to real writing, often contain more than one kind of error at once.
- Not every unusual sentence is an error — some are deliberate **stylistic choices**.
- Explaining *why* an error is wrong, using the correct grammar term, is as important as fixing it.

## 30. TEACHER SUPPORT NOTE

Likely misconception: Students often treat editing as a single pass ("read once, fix what you see") rather than a repeatable, category-by-category process, which means errors outside the category they happen to notice first are frequently missed.

Support strategy: Model the checklist explicitly on a shared class passage, checking it aloud five separate times (once per category) before moving on, so students see that thoroughness — not speed — catches the most errors.

Extension: Introduce peer editing using the same checklist, since a second reader often catches errors the original writer has become "used to" seeing.

Challenge: Ask advanced learners to write a passage that is grammatically correct but stylistically weak (choppy, vague, repetitive) and discuss why the checklist alone would not flag it as "wrong," connecting back to Chapter 20's sentence-craft skills.
