## 1. PEDAGOGICAL PURPOSE

**Why is this concept being taught here?**
Learners now enter Unit 7, which shifts focus from individual words to whole sentences. Having built strong vocabulary and grammar accuracy, they are ready to learn how to make their sentences richer, more varied, and more precise — key skills for effective writing.

**What should the learner be able to do with it?**
The learner can:
- Expand a simple sentence by adding relevant details.
- Combine short, choppy sentences into one smoother sentence.
- Remove unnecessary repetition from a sentence.
- Arrange words in natural, correct order.
- Choose precise words over vague ones.
- Vary sentence length and structure in their own writing.

## 7. DISCOVER / EXPLICIT TEACHING

### Explicit explanation

Skilful writers use several tools to build better sentences:

| Tool | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Expansion | Adds detail (when, where, how, why) | "The dog barked." → "The big dog barked loudly at the gate." |
| Combination | Joins short sentences with connecting words | "I was tired. I went to bed." → "I was tired, so I went to bed." |
| Removing repetition | Cuts unnecessary repeated words | "The red red apple" → "The red apple" |
| Word order | Arranges words naturally | "Quickly ran the boy" → "The boy ran quickly" |
| Precision | Replaces vague words with exact ones | "The thing was nice" → "The gift was thoughtful" |

## 8. GRAMMAR WORD

## Grammar Word: SENTENCE EXPANSION

**Sentence expansion** means adding extra, relevant detail to a simple sentence to make it more informative and interesting, usually by answering questions like *when, where, how,* or *why*.

### Examples

- Simple: "The bird sang."
- Expanded: "Early in the morning, the small bird sang sweetly from the tree."

### Non-examples / Contrast

- Adding unrelated or unnecessary detail is not true expansion: "The bird, which reminds me of my lunch, sang." (irrelevant detail)

### Quick Check

Expand this sentence with one detail of "when": "The children played." *(e.g., "After school, the children played.")*

---

## 9. CORE EXPLANATION

**Combining short sentences**
→ Example: "It was raining. We stayed inside." → "It was raining, **so** we stayed inside."
→ Contrast: Joining words like *and, but, so, because, when, although* each create a slightly different relationship between ideas.
→ Check: Does the connecting word correctly show how the two ideas relate (reason, contrast, time)?

**Removing repetition**
→ Example: "The tall, tall boy climbed the tall tree." → "The tall boy climbed the tree."
→ Contrast: Repeating a word for emphasis in speech ("So, so happy!") is different from accidental repetition in writing, which usually should be removed.
→ Check: Is the repeated word adding meaning, or just cluttering the sentence?

**Correct word order**
→ Example: "Quickly the girl the ball threw." (incorrect order) → "The girl quickly threw the ball." (natural order)
→ Contrast: English usually follows Subject–Verb–Object order, with describing words placed close to the words they describe.
→ Check: Does the sentence sound natural when read aloud?

**Precision over vagueness**
→ Example: "The thing was nice." → "The gift was thoughtful." / "The weather was nice." → "The weather was warm and sunny."
→ Contrast: Vague words like *thing, nice, good, stuff* can almost always be replaced with something more exact.
→ Check: Could I picture this clearly if I only heard this word?

---

## 10. TYPES / CLASSIFICATIONS

### Expansion Questions

Name: The 5 expansion questions
Meaning: Questions used to add detail to a simple sentence.
Example: When? Where? How? Why? Which one/whose?
Contrast: Not every sentence needs all five — choose the details that matter most.
Quick Check: Which question does "loudly" answer in "The dog barked loudly"? *(How?)*

### Joining Words for Combination

Name: Coordinating and subordinating joining words
Meaning: Words used to combine two sentences into one.
Example: and (addition), but (contrast), so (result), because (reason), when (time), although (contrast/concession)
Contrast: Choosing the wrong joining word can create a confusing or illogical sentence.
Quick Check: Which joining word best fits: "I was hungry, ___ I ate a sandwich"? *(so)*

## 11. WORKED EXAMPLE

**Sentences to improve:** "The cat sat. The cat was on the mat. The mat was soft."

Step 1: Identify the repeated subject — "the cat" and "the mat" appear more than once.

Step 2: Combine the ideas into one sentence, keeping only necessary detail.

Step 3: Choose a natural word order and remove repetition.

Answer: "The cat sat on the soft mat."

Why: Combining the three short sentences into one removes repetition, keeps all the important details (cat, sat, soft, mat), and reads far more smoothly.

## 12. COMPARE AND NOTICE

**Before:** "I woke up. I brushed my teeth. I ate breakfast. I went to school."
**After:** "After I woke up, I brushed my teeth, ate breakfast, and went to school."

What changed? Four short sentences were combined into one, using a joining word ("After") and a list structure ("brushed... ate... and went").
Why? The combined sentence flows more smoothly and shows the connection between the actions more clearly.

## 16. COMMON CONFUSION

### WATCH OUT!

Common confusion: Learners sometimes think that combining sentences means simply joining them with "and" every time, which can create a long, unclear "and... and... and" chain instead of a well-structured sentence.

How to check: Ask, "Does 'and' really show the right relationship, or would 'but,' 'so,' 'because,' or 'when' fit better?"

Correct examples: "I was tired, **so** I rested" (not just "I was tired **and** I rested," which loses the cause-and-effect meaning).

## 30. TEACHER SUPPORT NOTE

Likely misconception: Students often believe "better" sentences simply means "longer" sentences, leading to overloaded, run-on combinations instead of clear, well-built ones.

Support strategy: Emphasise that quality, not just length, matters — model reading combined sentences aloud to check they still sound clear and natural.

Extension: Introduce simple compound and complex sentence structures more formally as a bridge to later grammar study.

Challenge: Ask advanced learners to take a very long, overloaded sentence and split it into two clearer, well-built sentences instead.
